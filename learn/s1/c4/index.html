The chapter

Listen to this chapter—

Three chapters, three pieces. Chapter 1 was the forward pass: pixels in, ten probabilities out. Chapter 2 was the loss and the step: measure how wrong, and move every dial a little downhill. Chapter 3 was the backward pass: how every dial finds its own slope. This chapter puts them in a loop and runs it until the network works.

Every number here comes from re-running Chapter 1's training exactly, and recording what happened at every step.

It starts as a stranger

Before training, the network's 13,002 numbers are random. They're drawn from a bell curve whose width depends on how many inputs each neuron has. This is called He initialization, after Kaiming He, and it keeps signals from shrinking to nothing or blowing up as they pass through the layers. Picture each first-layer neuron's 784 weights as a 28×28 image and you see static.

Shown 10,000 test digits, this random network gets 8.7% right. Guessing blindly among ten would get 10%. It's a coin with ten sides.

Step 0: 8.7% right on 10,000 test digits

Two piles that never mix

MNIST comes in two piles: 60,000 training digits and 10,000 test digits, written by different people. The network learns only from the first pile. The second is kept aside and used only to measure it. That separation is the most important habit in machine learning: a network can score perfectly on examples it has memorized, so the only honest measure is examples it has never seen.

A handful at a time, never the same order twice

Each step takes a batch of 64 training digits. 938 batches cover all 60,000 once; that's one epoch. Our run did 8 epochs, 7,504 steps. Before every epoch the whole pile is shuffled. The first epoch began with an 8, a 9, a 3, a 2; the second in a completely different order. Shuffling makes sure no batch is all 1s or all 7s, and that the order of the digits never teaches the network something by accident.

The loop

Here is the whole of training, one step:

  • 1 · batch take the next 64 digits
  • 2 · forward ten probabilities for each (Chapter 1)
  • 3 · loss −log p of the right answers, averaged (Chapter 2)
  • 4 · backward all 13,002 slopes (Chapter 3)
  • 5 · step every dial moves a little against its slope (Chapter 2)
  • 6 · again

Our step was Adam with a learning rate of 0.002, the variant of gradient descent from Chapter 2 that smooths each dial's slope and scales its step. Nothing else is hidden. The script at the bottom of this page is this loop, line for line.

Watching it learn

The first steps change the most. After 16 steps, having seen about a thousand digits, the network is right 39.2% of the time. After 96 steps, 85.0%. It passes 90% at step 256 and ends the first epoch at 93.3%. The next seven epochs add less than two points: 95.2% at the end. Learning is fast, then slow; almost every training curve you'll ever see has that shape.

Test accuracy · step 0: 8.7% · step 16: 39.2% · step 96: 85.0% · epoch 1: 93.3% · epoch 8: 95.2%

Chapter 1's 7 is a test digit, so the network never trained on it. From step 32 on it reads it correctly every time, and just after the first epoch it's 99.6% sure.

And the static changes. Watch the first-layer neurons' weight images during training and strokes appear: bright where ink should be, dark where it shouldn't. Nobody drew them. They are what 7,504 small steps against the slope left behind.

Seen and unseen

After each epoch we measured the network on both piles. At the end it's right on 96.4% of the 60,000 digits it trained on and 95.2% of the 10,000 it never saw. The gap is small, which means it learned the shapes of digits, not the particular pages it studied.

After 8 epochs · training digits 96.4% · test digits 95.2%

When the gap grows, when training accuracy keeps rising but test accuracy stalls or falls, the network is memorizing. That's called overfitting. The standard defence is a third pile, a validation set, carved out of the training data. You check it after every epoch and keep the version of the network that did best there. The test set is then used once, at the very end, so it stays honest. The script for this chapter does exactly that.

What still fools it

95.2% right means 480 of the 10,000 test digits wrong. The most common mistake is a 7 read as a 2, 23 times, then a 7 read as a 9 and a 2 read as a 3, 22 times each. Some mistakes are made with complete confidence: an 8 it calls a 6 at 100%, a 3 it calls an 8 at 100%. Look at those digits and many really do look like something else.

480 wrong of 10,000 · 7 read as 2: 23 times · 7 as 9 and 2 as 3: 22 each

Our network is tiny: 16 neurons in each hidden layer. Bigger networks, and convolutional networks, which look at small patches of the image at a time, get past 99% on these digits. But the loop that trains them is this one.

Where this lives today

Pretraining a large language model is this loop. The batch is a few million tokens of text instead of 64 digits. The forward pass predicts each next token; the loss is the same −log p; the backward pass is the same chain rule, done by the library; the step is AdamW, Adam with weight decay. It runs for hundreds of thousands of steps on thousands of chips, usually seeing most of its data only about once, and a held-out set is watched the whole way, just as we watched our test digits.

Real runs add a few more habits: a learning rate that warms up and then decays, saved checkpoints so a crash doesn't lose weeks of work, and careful mixing of data sources. But the heart of it is six lines: batch, forward, loss, backward, step, again.

Season 1, complete

That's the whole of Season 1. A neuron multiplies, adds, and keeps what's above zero. The loss says how wrong. The gradient says which way is downhill, and backpropagation finds it for every dial at once. The loop does it again and again, until 13,002 random numbers read handwriting. Season 2 takes the same loop to language: how text becomes numbers, and how a transformer learns to predict the next word.

Not one moment. Every single step.

Build it yourself

The whole loop, one file.

Everything from Season 1 in one NumPy script: He initialization, a validation set carved from the training data, a fresh shuffle every epoch, forward, backward written out, Adam, keep-the-best on validation, and the test set used exactly once at the end. Put it next to train_net.py from Chapter 1.

Download train_loop.pytrain_net.py
# Python 3.10+
pip install numpy
python train_loop.py

# expected (a minute or two on a laptop CPU)
step     0  untrained: train 7.9%  validation 7.7%
step   860  epoch  1: train 92.4%  validation 91.5%  gap +0.9%  <- best so far
step  1720  epoch  2: train 94.0%  validation 93.5%  gap +0.6%  <- best so far
…
step  8600  epoch 10: train 96.7%  validation 95.1%  gap +1.6%  <- best so far

test accuracy (10,000 digits never used for anything until now): 95.0%   wrong: 496

Try this: change the hidden layers from 16 to 64 neurons (and the 16s in the shapes). Compare validation accuracy with the 16-neuron run, and watch the train/validation gap for signs of memorizing.
