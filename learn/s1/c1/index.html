Look at a handwritten seven. You recognise it before you've even thought about it. A computer can't look at anything. What it gets is a list of 784 numbers. This chapter is about what happens between those two things: how a network made of very simple parts turns 784 numbers into the answer “seven”.

By the end you'll know what a neuron computes, what weights and biases are, what ReLU means, and why this small piece of arithmetic sits inside everything from a digit reader to ChatGPT.

A picture is a grid of numbers

The seven in the video comes from MNIST, a collection of 70,000 handwritten digits that researchers put together in the 1990s. It's the traditional first dataset in machine learning: small enough to understand completely, hard enough to be interesting.

Each digit is a square of 28 × 28 tiny squares called pixels. 28 × 28 is 784. Each pixel is stored as one number, its brightness, from 0 for black to 1 for white, with greys in between.

Our 7 is test image #328. Only 137 of its 784 pixels are above zero; the other 647 are pure black.

That's everything the network receives. It has no idea what a line or a curve is. It only has these numbers.

Line them up: a vector

The network doesn't take the picture as a square. It takes one long list: the first row of 28 pixels, then the second row after it, and so on until all 784 are in a line. A list of numbers kept in a fixed order is called a vector, and we'll call this one x.

One consequence is worth noticing. Once the picture is flattened, the network isn't told which pixels were neighbours. Anything it knows about shape, it has to learn.

A neuron is a weighted vote

An artificial neuron is a tiny calculator with one job: look at all 784 numbers and produce one number back.

To do that, it keeps one number of its own for every pixel, called a weight. A weight is how much the neuron cares about that pixel, and in which direction. A positive weight means “brightness here is evidence for me”. A negative weight means “brightness here is evidence against me”. A weight near zero means “I don't care about this spot”.

The neuron multiplies every pixel by its weight and adds the 784 results together. That total is called the weighted sum. Because black pixels are zero, anything multiplied by them is zero too, so only the ink actually counts.

Then it adds one more number of its own, the bias. The bias sets how easily the neuron is convinced: a positive bias gives it a head start, a negative one makes it harder to win over. Written as maths, the whole thing is one line:

z = w · x + b

w is the neuron's 784 weights, x is the 784 pixels, and b is the bias. The dot means “multiply each pair, then add them all up”. The result, z, is the neuron's score for this image.

Neuron 6: positive contributions +9.18, negative −3.26, bias +0.007 → z = 5.93
Why “neuron”? Brain cells receive signals from many other cells and fire when enough arrive. Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts wrote down a mathematical version of that in 1943. The resemblance ends there: an artificial neuron is multiply, add, and one more step.

ReLU: the one rule that bends

That one more step is called an activation function. It decides what the neuron actually passes on. The one in this chapter is called ReLU, short for Rectified Linear Unit.

ReLU's rule fits in one sentence: if the score is negative, pass on zero; otherwise, pass it on unchanged. “Rectified” is an old electrical word; a rectifier lets current through in one direction only. “Linear” is because whatever does get through isn't changed.

Neuron 6: 5.93 → 5.93 · Neuron 10: −2.86 → 0 (silent for this 7)

Why bother with such a simple rule? Because without it, the network can only ever draw straight lines. Multiplying and adding, then multiplying and adding again, always collapses into one single multiply-and-add, no matter how many layers you stack. ReLU's bend at zero breaks that. Put many bent pieces together and you can build curves; put enough together and you can draw almost any shape you need, including the complicated boundary between “looks like a 7” and “looks like a 1”. Mathematicians proved this in 1989 and 1991; it's called the universal approximation result.

Best straight line: error 0.657 · the same curve with 9 bends: error 0.002

Layers, and one big multiplication

One neuron asks one question of the image. A layer is a group of neurons all looking at the same input, each with its own weights, so each asks a different question. Our first layer has 16 neurons.

Stack the 16 lists of weights on top of each other and you get a grid of numbers, 16 rows by 784 columns, called a matrix and written W. Now the whole layer is one line of maths: z = Wx + b, then ReLU on each result.

Multiplying a matrix by a vector is the single most common operation in all of AI. It's exactly what graphics cards, GPUs, are built to do very fast, which is why AI runs on them.

Our network has four layers of numbers: 784 pixels, then 16 neurons, then another 16, then 10 at the end. Each layer's output is the next layer's input. It's the same shape Grant Sanderson uses in 3Blue1Brown's But what is a neural network?, which is a wonderful companion to this chapter.

For this 7: 9 of 16 first-layer neurons fire; 14 of 16 in the second layer

Ten lights: the answer

The last layer has ten neurons, one for each digit from 0 to 9. Their raw scores go through softmax, a function that turns any list of scores into probabilities: numbers between 0 and 1 that add up to exactly 1. The biggest one is the network's answer. Softmax comes back in Chapter 2.

“7” with probability 0.995

Nobody wrote the weights

Count every weight and bias in this network and you get 13,002 numbers, called its parameters. No person chose a single one of them.

They started as random numbers. Then the network was shown 60,000 labelled training digits, 64 at a time, and after each group every parameter was nudged slightly in whichever direction made the network a little less wrong. That's training. It took 7,504 nudges: eight full passes through the training digits. Each full pass is called an epoch.

Test accuracy on 10,000 unseen digits: 93.3% after epoch 1 → 95.2% after epoch 8 (random guessing: 10%)

How does the network know which way to nudge each of 13,002 numbers? That's the question for the next two chapters: Chapter 2 is about the slope, which direction counts as “less wrong”; Chapter 3 is about backpropagation, how the network finds that direction for every number at once. Together they're how every neural network, including the largest ones, learns.

How we got here

None of this is new. The ideas in this chapter are decades old; what changed was scale, and one invention in 2017.

  • 1943McCulloch and Pitts describe a mathematical neuron.
  • 1958Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron learns its own weights from examples.
  • 1986Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams show backpropagation training multi-layer networks (Chapter 3).
  • 1998Yann LeCun's networks read handwritten digits; MNIST is published.
  • 2012AlexNet, trained on GPUs with a million images, wins the ImageNet contest by a wide margin. Deep learning takes off.
  • 2017Google researchers publish “Attention Is All You Need”, introducing the transformer.
  • 2018–20GPT, BERT, GPT-3: transformers trained on enormous amounts of text.
  • 2022ChatGPT: a transformer, further trained to follow instructions (Season 3).

So neural networks existed long before ChatGPT. What the transformer changed was how a network handles a sequence, such as the words of a sentence. It lets every word look directly at every other word to decide what matters, a mechanism called attention. And it trains efficiently on graphics cards, which made it practical to learn from vast amounts of text. That's Season 2.

Where this neuron lives today

Here's the part that connects this small digit reader to the chatbots you use. A transformer is built from layers stacked on top of each other, and every layer has two parts. The first is attention. The second is called a feed-forward network, and it is exactly what you just learned: multiply by a matrix of weights, add a bias, bend, and do it again.

The original 2017 transformer used ReLU for that bend. Many newer models use smoother cousins with names like GELU and SwiGLU, but the idea is the same. A large language model repeats this block across dozens of layers, with thousands of neurons in each and billions of parameters in total.

Multiply, add, keep what's above zero. That's the whole trick, repeated at enormous scale.

Build it yourself

Train the exact network from the video.

One Python file using only NumPy. No GPU needed. It downloads MNIST, checks the files, trains the 784 → 16 → 16 → 10 network for eight epochs with a fixed random seed, and writes out every number the video and this page use.

Download train_net.py
# Python 3.10+
pip install numpy
python train_net.py

# expected (a few minutes on a laptop CPU)
epoch 1 step 938 test acc 0.9333
…
epoch 8 step 7504 test acc 0.9520
hero test[328] p(7)=0.9949 star neuron 6 z=5.927

Try this: delete the ReLU so every layer is a straight multiply-and-add, retrain, and watch the accuracy fall. That's “the one rule that bends”, happening on your own machine.
