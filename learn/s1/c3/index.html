Chapter 2 ended on a promise. To take one step downhill, training needs the slope of every one of the network's 13,002 dials. The obvious way to find a slope is to nudge the dial and re-run the network: 13,003 runs for every single step. Real training gets all 13,002 slopes, exactly, for about the price of one more run. The idea is called backpropagation, and it's how every neural network you've heard of is trained.

It has one move, used over and over: start at the error and walk backwards, passing the blame from each layer to the one before it.

Start where it went wrong

We start with the same untrained network and the same 7 as Chapter 2. It answered four, giving the 4 18.8% and the 7 16.6%, for a loss of 1.80. The first question backpropagation asks: how should each of the ten output scores change to lower that loss?

For softmax followed by cross-entropy loss, the answer is beautifully simple. The slope of the loss with respect to each output score is p − y: what the network guessed, minus what was true, where y is 1 for the correct digit and 0 for the rest. We'll call it the blame, written δ (delta).

δ = p − y

For our 7 the blame is −0.834 at the 7 and small positive numbers everywhere else, the largest being +0.188 at the 4. A negative slope means raising that score lowers the loss. So the blame says: push the 7's score up, push every other score down, the 4's most of all.

Blame at the outputs: −0.834 at the 7 · +0.188 at the 4

A chain of links

Now take one dial deep inside the network: the weight connecting pixel 462, a bright pixel in the middle of the 7, to neuron 5 of the first layer. Its effect on the loss has to travel. The pixel feeds neuron 5, neuron 5 feeds the second layer, the second layer feeds the ten outputs, and the outputs feed the loss. Every link in that chain has a slope of its own.

The chain rule from calculus says what to do with them: the slope of the whole chain is the product of the slopes of its links. If turning a knob by 1 moves a gear by 3, and moving that gear by 1 moves a hand by 2, the knob moves the hand by 6.

Follow one path. At the 7's output the blame is −0.834. The weight from neuron 1 of the second layer into the 7 is 0.433. The weight from our neuron 5 into that neuron 1 is 0.531, and both neurons fired, so their gates let the signal through. The pixel itself is 1.00. Multiply along the path: −0.834 × 0.433 × 0.531 × 1.00 = −0.192.

But that's only one road from the dial to the loss. Neuron 5 reaches all ten outputs through the ten second-layer neurons that fired, so there are 100 paths. The multivariable chain rule says: multiply along each path, then add over all the paths. The total is −0.624. When we nudge that dial directly and re-run the network, the slope comes out as −0.624103: the same, to six decimals.

W1[5, 462] · 100 paths · chain rule −0.624 · nudging −0.624103

Why backwards

Tracing a hundred paths for every one of 13,002 dials would be hopeless. Here's the trick that makes it cheap: every path from every first-layer dial passes through the same few neurons. So instead of following paths, compute the blame at each neuron once, starting from the loss and moving backwards, and let every dial reuse it.

The blame arriving at a neuron is the sum of the blame of every neuron it feeds, each multiplied by the weight of the link between them: the same weights as the forward pass, used in reverse. Then it passes through the neuron's gate. Layer by layer, the blame flows from the loss back to the first layer. That backward flow is backpropagation.

δlayer = (Wᵀ δnext) ⊙ [z > 0]

It's the same sum over paths as before, just organised so that no multiplication is ever done twice. Computer scientists will recognise the pattern: it's dynamic programming.

Silent neurons, black pixels

Two things stop blame in its tracks. The first is the ReLU gate from Chapter 1. A neuron whose total came out negative outputs 0, and its slope there is 0 too, so whatever blame arrives at it goes no further. For our 7, only 7 of the 16 first-layer neurons fired, and 10 of the 16 in the second layer. The silent ones pass nothing back.

The second is the input itself. Once the blame reaches a neuron, the slope of each dial feeding it is simply the blame times whatever came in on that dial's link:

∂L/∂w = δ · x

A black pixel sends in 0, so its dials get a slope of 0 and won't move for this digit. Only 137 of the 7's 784 pixels have any ink. Put the gates and the black pixels together and, from this one 7, 1,156 of the 13,002 dials get a slope. The rest will hear from other digits, which is one reason training shows the network many examples at a time.

From one 7: 7/16 and 10/16 neurons fired · 137 ink pixels · 1,156 of 13,002 dials get a slope

The price of a slope

Count the work. One forward pass through our network takes 12,960 multiplications. One backward pass, blame plus every slope, takes 13,376. Together that's 26,336, and it buys all 13,002 slopes. Nudging each dial instead would take 13,003 forward passes, one to start and one per dial: 168,518,880 multiplications. Backpropagation is 6,399× less work, and gives the same numbers.

Forward 12,960 · backward 13,376 · nudging every dial 168,518,880 · 6,399× cheaper

The gap grows with the network. Nudging costs a full run per dial, so its price grows with the square of the network's size, while backpropagation stays a small, fixed multiple of one run. For a model with billions of dials, it's the difference between impossible and routine.

One step, then the loop

With every slope in hand, Chapter 2's step does the rest: move every dial a little against its slope. Take one step of size 0.1 using this one 7's slopes, and the untrained network's loss on it falls from 1.80 to 0.073; it now gives the 7 93.0%. That's one digit and one step, and a network that learned only this would simply memorise it.

Real training runs the full loop: forward on a batch of 64 digits, loss, backward, step, and round again. Our network went round 7,504 times. At the end the same 7 has a loss of 0.005 and its blame is 43× smaller than at the start. There's almost nothing left to fix.

One step on this 7: loss 1.80 → 0.073 · trained: 0.005, blame 43× smaller

Where this lives today

Nobody writes backward passes by hand any more. Libraries like PyTorch and JAX record every operation in the forward pass, then walk them in reverse, applying the chain rule to each one. This is called reverse-mode automatic differentiation, and it's backpropagation for any program, not just neural networks. When you call loss.backward() in PyTorch, this chapter is what runs.

In a large language model the backward pass costs roughly twice the forward pass, because at every layer it computes two things: the blame for the layer below, and the slopes of that layer's own dials. That's where a well-known rule of thumb comes from: training costs about 6 operations per parameter per token, 2 forward and 4 backward.

The idea was found more than once. Seppo Linnainmaa described reverse-mode differentiation in his 1970 master's thesis. Paul Werbos proposed using it to train neural networks in 1974. And in 1986 David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams showed it could teach networks useful internal features, the paper that made backpropagation famous.

We now have every piece: a forward pass, a loss, a backward pass, and a step. Chapter 4 puts them together into a complete training loop and runs it, from random numbers to a working network.

Backprop by hand, checked by nudging.

One NumPy file. It rebuilds the Chapter 1 network from the same random numbers, runs our 7 forward, walks the blame back with every line visible, checks five random slopes against nudging, counts the multiplications, and then trains a full epoch using nothing but its own backward pass. Put it next to train_net.py from Chapter 1.
