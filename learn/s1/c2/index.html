Follow the Slope

How a network that starts out as 13,002 random numbers learns: measure how wrong it is, find which way is downhill, and take a small step. Then do it again, seven thousand times.

At the end of Chapter 1, our network looked at a handwritten seven and said “seven”, 99.5% sure. It did that with 13,002 numbers that no person chose. Every one of them started out random. This chapter is about the idea that turned those random numbers into a working network, and it's the same idea that trains every large model you've heard of.

It has three parts. Measure how wrong the network is. Work out which way to change each number so it becomes a little less wrong. Then change them all a little in that direction, and repeat.

Before it learned anything

To see where training starts, we rebuilt the exact same network with the exact same random starting numbers and showed it the same seven from Chapter 1. Its answer: four. It gave the four 18.8% and the seven 16.6%. Everything else got something in between.

Those percentages come from softmax, which you met at the end of Chapter 1. The last layer produces ten raw scores; softmax turns them into ten probabilities that are all positive and add up to 1. An untrained network's scores are close to random, so its probabilities are spread out, and whichever digit wins does so by accident.

Untrained, shown our 7: “4” at 18.8% · the 7 at 16.6%

One number for how wrong

To improve something, you first need to measure it. Training needs a single number that says how wrong the network is. That number is called the loss. Low loss means good; the whole job of training is to make it go down.

For a network that picks between answers, the standard loss looks at just one number: p, the probability the network gave to the correct answer. The loss is minus the logarithm of p. This is called cross-entropy loss.

loss = −log p

The logarithm does something useful here. If the network is sure and right, p is close to 1 and −log p is close to 0. If it gives the right answer only a tiny chance, p is close to 0 and the loss shoots up without limit. A confident wrong answer costs far more than a hesitant one. For our 7, the untrained network gave the right answer 0.166, so its loss is 1.80.

There's a useful landmark. A network that knows nothing and spreads its bets evenly gives every digit 1 in 10, and −log 0.1 is 2.30. Averaged over 10,000 test digits, our untrained network scores 2.38, almost exactly a blind guess. After training, the same 7 scores 0.005.

Our 7: loss 1.80 untrained · 2.30 = a blind guess · 0.005 trained

In practice the loss is averaged over a group of examples, not taken from one digit. But it's always the same idea: one number that goes down as the network gets more things right, and more confidently.

Thirteen thousand dials

Think of the network's 13,002 numbers as dials. Each one affects the answer a little. Training has to decide, for every dial, which way to turn it.

Here's the simplest possible way to find out: take one dial, turn it a tiny bit, and see what happens to the loss. We did this with the bias of neuron 6, Chapter 1's star neuron, using the first 64 training digits. It starts at 0.00, where the loss is 2.4219. Turn it to −0.10 and the loss drops to 2.4087. Turn it to +0.10 and the loss rises to 2.4371.

Neuron 6's bias: −0.10 → 2.4087 · 0.00 → 2.4219 · +0.10 → 2.4371

So turning this dial up makes things worse and turning it down makes things better. How much worse, per unit of turning, is the slope: the change in loss, 0.028, divided by the change in the dial, 0.2. That's 0.142. In calculus this is called a derivative; because we held every other dial still while we measured it, a partial derivative.

Every one of the 13,002 dials has its own slope. Put all 13,002 slopes in one list and you have the gradient, written g. It's the single most important object in machine learning: for every dial at once, it says which way is uphill for the loss, and how steep.

How the gradient is really found. Nudging dials one at a time works, but it would take 13,002 extra passes through the network for every single step. Real training computes all 13,002 slopes exactly, for about the cost of one extra pass, with an algorithm called backpropagation. For neuron 6's bias it gives 0.142, the same as our nudging. How it does that is Chapter 3.

Downhill: one line of maths

The gradient points uphill. We want to go down. So we move every dial a small step in the opposite direction of its slope. That's gradient descent, and it's one line:

w ← w − η g

w is any dial and g is its slope. The minus sign is what turns uphill into downhill: a dial with a positive slope gets turned down, one with a negative slope gets turned up, and a dial whose slope is nearly zero barely moves. η (eta) is the size of the step, called the learning rate.

That's the whole method: measure the loss, get the gradient, step against it, repeat. Our network did this 7,504 times. Its loss on training digits went from 2.38 to 0.14, and its accuracy on 10,000 digits it had never seen went from 8.7%, no better than guessing, to 95.2%.

The fine print. Our network was actually trained with Adam, the most widely used variant of gradient descent, with η = 0.002. Adam still steps against the gradient, but it smooths each dial's slope over recent steps (momentum) and scales each dial's step by how large its slopes have been, so every dial moves at a sensible pace. Nearly every large model today is trained with Adam or its relative AdamW.

Sixty-four at a time

To get the true gradient you'd have to run all 60,000 training digits through the network before every single step. That's slow. Instead, each step looks at a small random group called a batch. Ours used 64 digits per step. The gradient from 64 digits isn't exact; it's a noisy estimate of the true one. But it's more than 900 times cheaper, and it points roughly the right way.

This is stochastic gradient descent: stochastic just means random. The loss on each batch jumps around, but the trend goes steadily down. 938 batches of 64 make one pass over the training digits (one epoch); eight epochs made our 7,504 steps. The idea is older than neural networks: Herbert Robbins and Sutton Monro showed in 1951 that steps based on noisy estimates still arrive, provided the steps are small enough.

Too big, too small

The learning rate is the one knob you always have to set, and it's easy to get wrong in both directions.

Too big, and each step overshoots: the network jumps across the valley it was trying to go down, lands higher on the far side, and jumps back even further. The loss bounces and can blow up completely. Too small, and every step is safe but tiny; after thousands of steps the network has barely moved. In the video, on a slice of our network's real loss surface, a step size of 45 settles into the valley, 89 bounces from wall to wall until it's thrown off the map, and 0.3 has hardly left the starting point.

Plain gradient descent, one epoch · η = 0.001: 42.0% · η = 0.1: 91.8% · η = 2: 10.1% (guessing)

Those last numbers come from the script at the bottom of this page. You can run it yourself and watch all three happen.

A landscape you can't see

Picture the loss as the height of a landscape. Every possible setting of the dials is a place, and the height there is how wrong the network is. Training is a walk downhill. The trouble is that our landscape has 13,002 directions to move in, one per dial. Nobody can picture that.

So researchers take slices: pick two directions and measure the real loss at every point of the flat sheet they span. In 2014, Ian Goodfellow and colleagues looked along the straight line from a network's starting point to its trained point. In 2018, Hao Li and colleagues drew two-dimensional slices, the source of the well-known pictures of loss landscapes.

The landscape in our video is one of these slices, computed from our own network. Its first direction runs from the untrained start to the trained end; its second passes through the point where the training path bends furthest from that line, at step 1,548. Every height is the network's true loss, measured on 2,000 training digits at 14,641 points, and the glowing path is the real training run, projected onto the slice.

Two honest caveats. A slice is a slice: the real path wanders slightly out of the sheet, and there are 13,000 other directions we aren't drawing. And in very high dimensions the intuition of getting stuck in a small dip mostly doesn't hold. Most flat spots in large networks turn out to be saddles, still downhill in some direction, which gradient descent eventually finds.

Where this lives today

Everything in this chapter scales up without changing. A large language model is trained on exactly this loop. Its job is to predict the next piece of text, called a token. Its loss is the same −log p, where p is the probability it gave to the token that actually came next, chosen from tens of thousands of possible tokens instead of ten digits. Its gradient has billions of entries instead of 13,002. Its batches hold millions of tokens instead of 64 digits. And it's trained with Adam, like ours.

There's one question we've skipped: how does the network get 13,002 slopes, or billions, in one go? Every dial's effect on the loss travels through a chain: from the dial, through the layers, out to the ten lights and into the loss. Chapter 3 follows that chain backwards.

Measure how wrong. Find the slope. Take a small step down. That's how every neural network learns.

Build it yourself

Three learning rates, one network.

One NumPy file. It starts the Chapter 1 network from the same random numbers and trains it for one epoch with plain gradient descent, w ← w − η g, at three step sizes. Every line of the update is visible. Put it next to train_net.py from Chapter 1.

Download follow_the_slope.pytrain_net.py
# Python 3.10+
pip install numpy
python follow_the_slope.py

# expected (under a minute on a laptop CPU)
eta = 0.001  loss every 100 steps: 2.42 2.27 2.21 … 1.62   test accuracy 42.0%
eta = 0.1    loss every 100 steps: 2.42 0.56 0.42 … 0.15   test accuracy 91.8%
eta = 2.0    loss every 100 steps: 2.42 2.31 2.33 … 2.29   test accuracy 10.1%

Try this: change the batch size from 64 to 1, then to 6,000, and compare how fast each learns per second. That's the trade-off behind every training run.
