Pieces of a Sentence

Music video“Pieces of a Sentence”

A computer holds only numbers, and a sentence isn’t one. Before a GPT can read a word, text has to be cut into pieces, every piece given a number, and every number given a place, a list of 128 numbers the model learns, where words used alike end up close together. This is the first layer of every language model, built from scratch on Shakespeare.

Season 1 taught a small network to read handwritten digits. Season 2 builds the kind of model behind ChatGPT, a GPT, one part at a time. Its first problem comes before any learning: a computer holds only numbers, and a sentence isn’t one.

So for this season we trained one small GPT, and every number in these chapters comes from it. It read Tiny Shakespeare, 1,115,394 characters from the plays. It has 875,264 parameters: 4 layers, 4 attention heads, 128 numbers for every piece of text, and it looks at up to 128 pieces at a time. It trained for 5,000 steps.

875,264 parameters · trained on 1,115,394 characters of Shakespeare

Letters are already numbers

The simplest way to turn text into numbers is to list every character that appears and number them. Tiny Shakespeare uses only 65: the line break is number 0, the space is number 1, then punctuation marks and symbols, then capital letters, then small ones. The space is the most common, 169,892 times; the line break appears 40,000 times. With this list the whole text becomes 1,115,394 numbers, and a model can learn to predict the next one.

That works; Andrej Karpathy’s famous char-rnn did exactly this. But it’s wasteful. Every word costs many steps, and a model that sees 128 characters at a time sees only a few lines of a play. Most of its effort goes on spelling.

Keep the pieces you use the most

The fix is called byte-pair encoding (BPE). Go through the whole text and count every pair of neighbouring characters. The most common pair in all of Shakespeare is a space followed by ·t, 23,837 times: the start of the, to, thou, that. Glue that pair into one new piece. Count again: now it’s h + e, 18,203 times. Glue it. Then ·a, 13,541 times; then ou, 12,730. Every merge is one new piece, built from two older ones.

We repeated that 447 times: 65 characters + 447 merges = a vocabulary of 512 pieces. Two rules shape them. The text is first split into words, so a piece never crosses from one word into the next. And a word keeps the space in front of it, as GPT-2 does, so ·say and say are different pieces. On this page a leading space is shown as a small dot.

65 characters + 447 merges = 512 pieces · first merges: ·t 23,837 · he 18,203 · ·a 13,541 · ou 12,730

One sentence, in pieces

Take the line the song keeps coming back to: I never learned to say it. That’s 25 characters. Our tokenizer cuts it into 10 pieces:

I·never·learned·to·say·it

15 of the 447 merges touch this sentence along the way. Common words like to, say and it are a single piece each; a rarer word like learned is built from four parts. Across the whole text, 1,115,394 characters become 516,604 pieces, 2.16 characters per piece on average. The same window of 128 pieces now holds more than twice as much Shakespeare.

“I never learned to say it”: 25 characters → 10 pieces, 15 merges · the whole text: 516,604 pieces, 2.16 characters each

A number is not a meaning

Each piece is now a number, its position in the list of 512. I is #21. ·say is #322. ·love is #324. But these numbers mean nothing: #323 is ·here and #325 is ·their. They sit next to love only because of the order the merges were learned.

So the model doesn’t use the number itself. It uses it to look up a row in a table: 512 rows, one per piece, each holding 128 numbers. That row is the piece’s embedding. The table is 512 × 128 = 65,536 numbers, about 7.5% of the whole model, and they start out random. They’re learned by the same loop as everything in Season 1: forward, loss, backward, step.

Think of the 128 numbers as a position. Two numbers place a point on a map; three place it in a room; 128 place it in a space we can’t picture, but where distance and direction work exactly the same way.

You shall know a word by the company it keeps

Nobody tells the model which words are alike. It’s only ever trained to predict the next piece. But pieces that appear in the same kinds of places, among the same kinds of words, get pushed toward similar positions, because that helps the prediction. The linguist J. R. Firth put it in 1957: you shall know a word by the company it keeps.

To measure how close two pieces are, compare the directions of their 128-number arrows: multiply them number by number, add it all up, and divide by both lengths. That’s cosine similarity. 1 means pointing the same way; 0 means unrelated.

cos(a, b) = a · b ⁄ (|a| |b|)

Here is what our trained model learned. The nearest pieces to ·say are think (0.74), then see, hear, tell, know, speak. To ·will: shall, must, should, can, may, would. To ·my: thy, the, your, his, her, our. To ·king: son, brother, father, lord. And a comma’s nearest neighbours are the other marks: ; ! . : ?

nearest to ·say: think 0.74 · see 0.57 · hear 0.56 · tell 0.55 · nearest to ·will: shall 0.68 · to ·my: thy 0.68 · to ·king: son 0.61

Love is the most interesting. Its nearest pieces are death (0.52) and life (0.51). Next comes ·hat, which in these plays is almost always the start of hate. Love and hate mean opposite things, but they keep the same company: I love thee, I hate thee. An embedding learns where a word is used, not what it means to us. It’s a tiny model that read one book, so its map is rough; but the pattern is the one the largest models learn too.

Watching the neighbourhoods form

We saved the whole embedding table 17 times during training. At step 0 the neighbours of ·say are nonsense: o Which - ·b. Of every piece’s 8 nearest neighbours, only 2% are the ones it will have at the end. By step 100 it’s 20%; by step 1,000, 67%; by step 4,000, 96%.

Meanwhile the loss falls. At step 0 it’s 6.26 on text the model never trained on: what blind guessing among 512 pieces gives, ln 512 = 6.24. By the end it’s 3.06. The model got better at predicting the next piece, and the neighbourhoods are a side effect of getting better.

nearest 8 already final · step 0: 2% · step 100: 20% · step 1,000: 67% · step 4,000: 96% · held-out loss 6.26 → 3.06
About the galaxy in the video. 128 dimensions can’t be drawn, so we squeezed them into 3 with t-SNE, a method that tries to keep each point’s nearest neighbours nearby. It keeps 55% of each piece’s 8 nearest, against 19% for a plain projection (PCA). Treat the map as a sketch: the real closeness lives in all 128 numbers.

Say it backwards

There’s one more thing a sentence has that a list of pieces doesn’t: order. So we asked the trained model to read our line and measured how surprised it is by each piece, the same −log p loss from Season 1. In order, the average is 3.04, roughly as unsure as choosing among 21 equally likely pieces. Some pieces it almost knows: after ·lear, it gives n about a 98% chance.

Then we fed it the same 10 pieces backwards. The average surprise is 9.03, worse than a blind guess among all 512 (6.24). Hamlet’s To be, or not to be, that is the question: 3.50 in order, 7.54 backwards. Same pieces, same embeddings; only the order changed.

average surprise per piece · “I never learned to say it”: 3.04 in order, 9.03 reversed · “To be, or not to be…”: 3.50, 7.54 · blind guess 6.24

So how does the model know where a piece is standing? The part that reads the sentence, attention (the next chapter), looks at all the pieces at once and on its own has no sense of order. So every slot, 0 to 127, gets its own 128 learned numbers too: a position embedding, another 128 × 128 = 16,384 numbers. What the model reads at each slot is the piece’s embedding plus the slot’s: ·say in slot 8 is the 128 numbers of say plus the 128 numbers of position 8, added together.

inputt = embedding(piece) + position(t)

Where this lives today

Every large language model starts exactly like this. GPT-2’s tokenizer is byte-pair encoding with 50,257 pieces. It starts from the 256 possible bytes rather than from letters, so any text at all can be written, in any language, even emoji: 256 bytes + 50,000 merges + 1 end-of-text marker. Our 65 + 447, at scale. Newer models use vocabularies of 100,000 pieces or more; Llama 3’s has 128,256.

The embeddings grow too: ours have 128 numbers, the smallest GPT-2 had 768, GPT-3 had 12,288. And pieces explain some famous quirks. Ask a model how many r’s are in strawberry and it has to answer about letters it never directly sees. Text in languages that were rare in the tokenizer’s training data gets cut into more, smaller pieces, so it costs more and fits less in the window.

The same idea powers search. Embedding models turn whole sentences into vectors, and databases such as Postgres with the pgvector extension find the nearest ones by cosine similarity. That’s how retrieval finds the right documents to hand a model: company, measured as angles.

From a letter to a number, from a number to a place. Next: attention, how every piece looks at every other piece and decides which ones matter.

    Build it yourself

    A tokenizer, from scratch, in one file.

    The season’s tokenizer with no libraries: split into words, count pairs, merge the most common, repeat. It downloads Tiny Shakespeare once, learns the same 447 merges in the same order as our model, and cuts any sentence you give it into pieces.

    Download bpe.py
    # Python 3.10+, nothing to install
python bpe.py
python bpe.py "your own sentence"

# expected (about 15 seconds)
text: 1,115,394 characters, 65 distinct
merge   1:  ·t   (23,837 times)
merge   2:  he   (18,203 times)
…
merge 447:  UCH  (233 times)
65 characters + 447 merges = 512 pieces
the whole text: 516,604 pieces (2.16 characters per piece)

'I never learned to say it': 25 characters -> 10 pieces
  pieces: I | ·ne | ver | ·l | ear | n | ed | ·to | ·say | ·it
  ids:    [21, 228, 184, 90, 216, 52, 124, 96, 322, 146]

    Try this: change VOCAB = 512 to 1024, then 4096. Watch whole words appear as single pieces, and the characters per piece climb. Then type a word with an accent, like “café”, and see why GPT-2 starts from bytes instead of letters.
